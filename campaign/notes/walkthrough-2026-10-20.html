1. The question

What this campaign is trying to find out

Chang Sun published a transformer jet tagger for the CMS Level-1 trigger (arXiv:2510.24784) together with its code. His paper reports accuracy against a hardware-cost count. Your thesis is that a tagger whose weights are only −1 and +1 (binary weights) can sit on or above his accuracy-versus-cost curve.

This campaign is at N = 8, eight particles per jet, not the N = 64 of the earlier campaigns. Chang's paper reports every architecture at N = 8, 16, 32 and 64; its headline numbers (MHA 77.9 %, Linformer 79.8 %) are the N = 64 rows, and N = 8 is the smallest row (MHA 66.3 %). You chose N = 8 on 16 October as question Q13, one of three options: "N=8 first, against Chang's MHA-8 hardware row; N=64 may follow later" (decisions.md). The study then scopes the claim to the released two-head MHA at N = 8 and says it does not concern other N (STUDY line 206). The practical reasons on record are that the released example code is what CH-8 reruns exactly, and that N = 8 is the cheapest to train: the study's planning rate of 3 GPU-s per epoch is an N = 8 figure, while the per-epoch ceilings it borrowed from N = 64 models were 31–38.8 s (STUDY §10). A result here is a result at N = 8 only.

The comparator is Chang's own code rerun on our data split, called CH-8. His paper reports 66.3 % top-1 accuracy for that row (Table 1 row 1; STUDY §8.10). The study treats that figure as context, not a binding target, because his rows differ in heads, recipe and test usage; the campaign's real comparison is against our own CH-8 rerun.

The arms (STUDY §5)

  • CH-8: Chang's released code, rerun on our split. The comparator.
  • F000: our own reconstruction of Chang's model. If F000 and CH-8 disagree, our rebuild is wrong, and that is a stop (STUDY §14).
  • F111: our model with binary weights in all three layer groups (attention, feed-forward, hidden head). Each group can be on or off, which gives the factorial.
  • BF and FB forms: two ways to combine batch normalization with binarization at four fused sites. FB folds the normalization scale into the weights and then binarizes (one magnitude per output column). BF binarizes first (one magnitude per layer) and then folds. The hardware cost differs between them.

How cost is counted (STUDY §3.1–§3.4)

Cost is a bit-operation count of the exported constants. H1 counts the exact widths. H2 is lower: it assumes the hardware can apply one shared magnitude per column as a scale after a sign-only sum. H2 is only allowed as the headline if a real firmware export of a trained F111 model on mulder meets three criteria: bit-exact C-simulation on 4,096 validation jets, timing met (worst negative slack at least 0, initiation interval 1), and strictly fewer LUTs than the normal export. That is the export check. Your split-form rule (19 October 16:28 UTC): if both forms pass, H2 for all binary arms; if both fail, H1; if one passes, every binary arm trains under H1 and the passing form's arms also under H2, which adds 24 runs.

2. Findings

What has actually been found

In this campaign: engineering findings only

  • A real Keras 3.14 bug (ticket B10-F1). Keras tracks a layer's loss terms by Python object id and keeps stale ids between training traces. When an id is reused, one layer's beta × EBOPs penalty is silently dropped from the training step while the logged cost looks normal. It showed up as two of about 40 fresh PC runs diverging. Without the fix every binary result would have been trained without its cost penalty and nothing in the logs would have said so. A guard now empties stale ids and counts loss terms every step, on every run, CH-8 included (your decision B10F1-1, 19 October). Source: C/preflight/investigations/B10-F1.md.
  • Two fresh training runs on the PC CPU do not produce bit-identical records (gates G46 and G48, CH-8 at one resume boundary; G48 also fails on the PC GPU). This matters only for the kill-and-resume guarantee: if the cluster evicts a pod, can the run continue from a checkpoint as if nothing happened. The cause is not yet known. Source: C/preflight/gates/G46 and G48/29a459ce7ee6.
  • A possible read of the held-out test file during a gate run (G13 on the PC GPU). A file-watch saw a placeholder test file opened during one run but could not say by which process; the runner's own file table did not hold it; the CPU version of the same gate passes. If a real read, it is a stop under STUDY §14. Not yet resolved. A second audit of the same kind (G51 on the PC CPU, seen only after the summary was regenerated) counted one forbidden event during the seed draw while its kernel-level audit saw zero opens; also unresolved.
  • The export check has its outcome. FB (factored / normal): bit-exact on 4,096 validation jets, II = 1, worst negative slack −1.090 / −1.087 ns, post-opt CLB LUTs 394,351 / 402,252, so the factored route is smaller but neither route meets timing; criterion 2 fails. BF and the trained CH-8 normal route never completed C-synthesis at the 8 h or 24 h cap (rc 124), so BF has no result and fails by the study's fail-closed rule and the owner's record. Both forms fail, so H1 is the headline for every binary arm under your split-form rule; no extra H2 runs. Also recorded: at this model size the designs exceed the xczu7ev part in this out-of-context flow (LUT utilization 171 % and 175 %; Chang's own CH-8 at initialization 252 %), context for the report, not a stop. Sources: C/preflight/export/PREFLIGHT-rows.md, the evaluation report, STUDY v0.18 §3.4.

6. Time

Why it has taken this long

The honest accounting has three parts: the process, the orchestration's own mistakes, and the one thing that justifies the rigour.

The process

  • The lab rules (CLAUDE.md, RULES.md, SYSTEM.md) send every campaign through STUDY, PREFLIGHT, RUN, VERIFY, REPORT with reviewers and gates at each step. They were written after real problems: invented numbers, unreproducible runs, a cluster filled with our ConfigMaps. They are your rules and you can change them.
  • On this campaign the machinery grew past its purpose: a 2,126-line study in fifteen versions over four days, 202 commits (163 on one day), 86 gates in 21 batches, 130 check rows, and every fix requiring a new commit and a ~6 h gate pass.
  • Your standing instruction was to keep going ("automate everything overnight", "delegate all tasks to codex astra agents, keep going"). The machine did. Nobody stopped to ask whether the rigour level matched what you wanted. That is what this page is for.
  • Structural costs: context moves only through files, so each new session re-verifies the last; agents cannot see each other; sessions end at usage limits mid-step; the firmware synthesis is 24 h per attempt; the cluster needs a human approval that the harness could not even record until a BRIEF exists.

The orchestration's own errors, from the record

  • 17 October: a blinding slip by the orchestrator, recorded in decisions.md.
  • 18 October: gate G54 failed on seven power-table cells; the cause was an error in the study itself (fixed in v0.9/v0.10).
  • 19 October: the Codex operator forced WANDB_MODE=disabled, which failed G14 and cost a rerun; the orchestrator overwrote a G51 result file by reusing an output path (the original stdout was kept, a note left).
  • Reviews were run on a study that then changed because of decisions that could have been asked earlier (v0.11 was an ITERATE with 3 A-level findings after your decisions were written in).
  • The plan for tonight included two three-round Opus debates over whether a test-harness artifact is real. That is a lot of spend for a question a short look could settle.
  • Tonight's reboot killed two jobs with no automatic restart, because the only monitors were processes on the Mac.

What the rigour has bought

The Keras bug B10-F1. It would have trained every binary model without its cost penalty while the logs looked normal, and the campaign would have reported a false front. The gate that caught it (fresh-run agreement) is the same family that is failing now for a different reason. The held-out isolation gates are the other half: they are what makes a "beats Chang" claim defensible.

9. Glossary

Terms used above

EBOPs
Effective bit operations, the hardware-cost proxy from Chang's HGQ quantizer. "Native" is its own count; H1 and H2 are the campaign's exported-constant counts.
HGQ / HGQ2
High-granularity quantization: every value learns its own bit width during training, and can be pruned to zero.
da4ml, hls4ml
da4ml turns constant matrices into adder trees; hls4ml turns a network into FPGA firmware code. Vitis HLS makes hardware from that code; Vivado synthesizes it and reports LUTs and timing.
Held-out set
Test jets that no code may read until model selection is frozen. The G13 family guards it.
Kill-and-resume
Restarting a run from a checkpoint after the cluster evicts it, with identical records afterwards. The G46/G48 family tests it.
PID controller
The feedback loop that holds a run at its cost target during training.
Gate
An automated check with a pass/fail result and saved evidence; a launch needs the gates of record to pass or to be excluded with a reason.
BRIEF, ADMISSION, APPROVAL
The campaign's authority files. BRIEF says what may launch and under which limits; ADMISSION lists the admitted configs; APPROVAL is written only by your harness command.
B18, B19
Preflight batches. B18 is the final-commit gate pass; B19 is the cluster-side batch after approval (draws, smoke, resume, benchmark).
